
BrAInOS — does experience transfer between entities?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  `compound` showed that one entity gets better with experience. §2.4
  says the fleet gets better together and §6 sells a skill marketplace;
  both assume what one entity worked out is worth something to another.

  It has an obvious failure mode. What an entity learns is not "walking
  needs carpet", it is "walking needs carpet HERE" — and nothing in the
  claim says where here is. A robot in a carpeted workshop and one in a
  tiled kitchen learn opposite rules and both are right. Sell one to the
  other and you have sold a confident error.

  Pre-registered before the run: matched > alone > blind, and blind
  recovers eventually, because what is adopted is evidence rather than a
  verdict. The number that matters is how long the recovery takes.

  The ordering held. The recovery did not — under the first adoption
  rule, which copied the seller's evidence wholesale, a badly-sourced
  skill left the entity permanently worse than one that started from
  nothing: 80% against alone's 87%, still falling short after twelve
  hundred episodes, with the cost showing up as refusal rather than
  failure. The mechanism is the one skill.rs already warned about in its
  own comment: an entity that believes a condition is required declines
  exactly the situations that would disprove it.

  So the rule changed, and this is the run under the new one. What
  transfers is the *claim*, not the seller's numbers; the buyer holds it
  on trust, acts on it — which is what makes a skill worth buying — and
  deliberately violates it one blocked opportunity in twelve until it
  has five local tests of its own. Both numbers are below.

  36 entities across 3 deployments, 1200 episodes each

  What each deployment's veteran had to sell, after 600 attempts:
    carpeted workshop    carpet 90% over 52 tests, battery_ok 88% over 51 tests  (the true rule here is `carpet`)
    tiled kitchen        tile 87% over 57 tests, battery_ok 84% over 64 tests  (the true rule here is `tile`)
    loading bay          battery_ok 89% over 37 tests, unloaded 90% over 63 tests  (the true rule here is `unloaded`)

  Judgement by arm, over the entity's life (% of situations handled well):
                                         0-200 200-400 400-600 600-800800-10001000-1200
    alone                                  80%     84%     85%     86%     86%     87%
    adopts from the same deployment        85%     88%     87%     89%     88%     89%
    adopts from a random deployment        77%     83%     83%     86%     86%     87%
    handed the true rule (ceiling)         89%     89%     89%     89%     89%     90%

  Cost of a wrong belief — how often it refused something that would
  have worked (% of declines):
                                             1       2       3       4       5       6
    alone                                  15%     14%     13%     13%     12%     12%
    adopts from the same deployment        11%     10%     10%     10%     10%     10%
    adopts from a random deployment        18%     15%     14%     12%     12%     12%

Result
────────────────────
  final judgement   alone 87%  ·  matched 89%  ·  blind 87%  ·  ceiling 90%
  first slice       matched 85%  ·  blind 77%  ·  alone 80%
  probes            matched 4 · blind 4 deliberate violations per entity, 
                    which is what the correction costs

  A badly-sourced skill now stops costing anything after about 600 episodes
  — that is where a blind adopter is doing as well as a fresh entity is
  doing at the same age — and reaches the level a fresh entity ends up at
  by 1000. Under the old rule it reached neither, ever.

  What it costs: matched lost about a point against the old rule (89%
  where copying the seller's evidence gave 90%). That is the price of
  checking a claim that happened to be true. Under the old rule the same
  mechanism made a claim that happened to be false permanent.

  What this cannot show: the three deployments are three rules I wrote.
  It says what the mechanism does when worlds differ. It does not say how
  much real deployments differ, and anyone quoting the number without
  that sentence is misquoting it.
