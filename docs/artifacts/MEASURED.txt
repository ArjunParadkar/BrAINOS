What the numbers mean
─────────────────────
  A gate evaluation is 118.4 us and 59% of it is the two ed25519 operations 
  — one to sign the proposal, one to verify it. Everything else the eight 
  stages do, including the body's forward model and the Merkle append, is 
  the remaining 41%. 

  That has a consequence for where the gate runs. ed25519 in software on an 
  Xtensa core at 240 MHz is a few milliseconds per operation, so the same 
  evaluation on the board is of order 10 ms — which means a body cannot 
  gate at its own loop rate. This is not a performance problem to optimise 
  away; it is the reason Tier 0 runs inside a pre-authorized envelope 
  instead of asking per step. The measurement agrees with the architecture 
  rather than the other way round, which is the useful direction. 

  One reflex pass is 42 ns, and re-deriving the tip-over margin from scratch 
  would add 179 ns. A 1 kHz servo loop has a millisecond; both together are 
  under a percent of it, on this machine. Scaled by the hundred-fold an 
  Xtensa core is slower, still under one percent. 

  Retrieval over a graph at its episodic bound is 1.25 ms, about 721x the 
  cost of writing one memory. That ratio is what makes rebuilding the index 
  on read rather than maintaining it on write the right trade: reads are 
  rare and thinking is what they feed, writes are constant and happen while 
  something is moving. 

  Sealing the whole state graph into a journal record is 1.59 ms. At that 
  cost, committing on every episode would be wasteful and committing only at 
  sleep would lose a session to a power cut — which is what the segmenting 
  is for: episodes commit often and cheaply, the skill graph and the life 
  record rarely. 


What each one actually does
───────────────────────────
  KIRA: one full eight-stage evaluation (granted)
      sign, verify, authorize against the body map, validate against the schema, simulate through the body policy, check the ruleset, mint a token, append to the Merkle log
  KIRA: one evaluation refused at authorize
      the cheap path: a verb no limb advertises never reaches validate or simulate
  ed25519: one signature
      what the gate pays per grant, and per journal record
  ed25519: one verification
      what the gate pays per proposal, and the body pays per session handshake
  hexapod: plan and verify a whole gait cycle
      48 poses, each through forward kinematics, a convex-hull support polygon, the servo table with its linked pairs, and the rail-current budget
  hexapod: the tip-over check alone, over a whole cycle
      how much of planning is the stability forward model
  hexapod: the tip-over check on one pose
      what the reflex layer would pay if it re-checked every sample from scratch
  bodyd: one reflex pass
      watchdogs, the link, every measured channel against the envelope, one sample applied, telemetry assembled
  memory: one recall over a full graph
      BM25 over a graph at its episodic bound, re-ranked by salience, confidence, origin and recency
  memory: writing one episode
      into a graph that is already at its bound, so the prune path runs
  memory: one consolidation pass
      crystallize, promote, chain and forget over a session's episodes
  journal: seal one record
      hash, sign and frame a whole state graph for the two-slot journal
  wire: seal one frame
      header, HMAC-SHA256 truncated to 128 bits
  wire: open one frame
      magic, length, constant-time MAC comparison

Does it fit on the board?
─────────────────────────
  An ESP32-S3 has 512 KiB of SRAM. Allowing 320 KiB for the firmware, the
  gait loop's buffers and the wire, a state graph has about 192 KiB.

  default bounds (4,096 episodes)      needs  492 KiB of  192 KiB   DOES NOT FIT
  embedded bounds (256 episodes)       needs   30 KiB of  192 KiB   fits

  This is why Bounds::EMBEDDED exists and is not a smaller number chosen
  for tidiness. An entity on the board remembers less and consolidates
  more often; the same entity on a workstation body remembers more. It is
  the same entity either way — the bound is a property of the body it is
  currently thinking in, not of who it is.


  a state graph with 1001 memories encodes to 123064 bytes — about 123 B per memory,
  so an entity at its default episodic bound of 4,096 episodes is roughly
  492 KiB of durable state.
